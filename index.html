Statistical Studies, Variables & Sampling

S1 · S2 · S3 · 7 questions

The answer

Parameter: a numerical summary that describes the entire population. It's usually unknown because we rarely measure everyone. Uses Greek letters: μ (mean), σ (standard deviation), p (proportion).

Statistic: a numerical summary that describes the sample — the group we actually measured. Uses Roman letters: x̄ (sample mean), s (sample SD), p̂ (sample proportion).

🧠 Memory anchors — "P and P, S and S":
Parameter describes the Population.
Statistic describes the Sample.

Also: parameters use Greek letters (from ancient Greece — far away, unknown). Statistics use Roman/Latin letters (close, measurable).
⚠️ Common mistake: Calling a number from a survey a "parameter" because it sounds more formal. The deciding question is always: Was every member of the population of interest measured? If you only surveyed a subset → it's a statistic, regardless of how large the sample is.
Try Statistic vs. Parameter — practice identifying which is which

The answer

Population: the complete group the researcher wants to draw conclusions about. Signal phrases: "all adults in…," "every SAC student," "all customers." It's the group you wish you could measure.

Sample: the subset of the population that was actually measured. Signal phrases: "200 students were surveyed," "a random selection of 50 records." It's who you did measure.

Sampling frame: the list or source from which the sample was drawn. It's what you could have selected from — and it might not perfectly match the population. Example: using a phone directory as the frame to study "all city residents" excludes people without listed numbers.

🧠 Three-question triage: (1) Who do we want to know about? → Population. (2) Who did we actually measure? → Sample. (3) Where did our subjects come from? → Sampling frame.
⚠️ Common mistake: Describing the sample too specifically or too broadly. If the study surveyed "people who had their blood tested at Clinic A," the sample is those specific patients — not "blood samples" (the wrong unit) and not "all adults" (too broad). Match the unit of observation precisely.

The answer

Stratified sampling: Divide the population into groups (strata) based on a shared characteristic (e.g., class year). Then randomly select some members from each group. Every stratum is represented.

Cluster sampling: Divide the population into groups (clusters), usually based on geography or convenience (e.g., classrooms). Then randomly select entire groups and survey everyone in those selected groups. Only some clusters are included.

Side-by-side comparison

Groups defined byA shared characteristic (age, major, income)Geography or convenience (classrooms, zip codes)
Who gets selected?Some from every groupAll from a few selected groups

🧠 Mnemonic: Stratified = Sprinkle (pick a few from each group). Cluster = Collect (grab entire groups). Or: strata are like layers of a cake — you get a slice from every layer. Clusters are like scooping a few whole cakes out of a bakery.

The answer

Ask this one question: "Would the average of this variable make meaningful sense?"

Quantitative: Numbers where arithmetic is meaningful — weight, height, time, score, temperature, income. "Average height = 5'7"" makes sense.

Categorical: Labels, groups, or categories — even when they look like numbers. Zip code, phone number, jersey number, student ID, gender, major, yes/no responses. "Average zip code = 92705.3" is meaningless.

🧠 The average test: "Would the average of this variable mean anything?" If yes → quantitative. If no → categorical. A zip code average is nonsensical → categorical. An exam score average is meaningful → quantitative.
⚠️ The numeric trap: Not all numbers are quantitative. Zip codes, phone numbers, jersey numbers, social security numbers, and student IDs are all categorical — they are labels disguised as numbers. The test is meaningfulness of arithmetic, not whether digits are involved.

The answer

Explanatory variable (X): the variable the researcher believes may explain or predict the other. It's the potential cause, the input, the predictor. Look for: what the researcher is manipulating, controlling, or using as the "because" variable.

Response variable (Y): the outcome being measured — what we think might change or be predicted. Look for: what the researcher is trying to understand, measure, or predict.

Signal phrasing: "Does [X] affect [Y]?" → X is explanatory, Y is response. "Is [Y] related to [X]?" → same logic. "Researchers studied whether [X] leads to [Y]" → X is explanatory.

🧠 Ask the question: "What is the researcher trying to predict or explain?" That's the response variable. "What are they using to predict it?" That's the explanatory variable. The response variable is always what you're trying to understand.
⚠️ Common mistake: Assuming explanatory = cause and response = effect. In observational studies, explanatory and response labels reflect the structure of the research question, not proven causation. Two variables can be labeled explanatory/response without any causal relationship established.

The answer

A lurking (confounding) variable is something not included in the study that is related to both the explanatory and response variables — making it look like X causes Y when something else is really driving both.

To spot one, ask: "Is there a third factor that could cause both X and Y to move together?"

Classic example: ice cream sales and drowning rates both go up in summer. But ice cream doesn't cause drowning — hot weather is the lurking variable driving both. Removing hot weather would break the apparent connection.

🧠 Quick checklist: (1) What else is going on in the context? (2) What lifestyle or environmental factor might connect these two variables? (3) If this third factor were removed, would the relationship disappear? If yes — that's your lurking variable.
Why lurking variables matter: They are why we can't claim causation from observational studies. Even a strong correlation could be entirely explained by a lurking variable. Only random assignment — which distributes lurking variables equally across groups — controls for them.

The answer

In an observational study, the researcher only watches — they do not assign participants to treatments. This means that any lurking variable could be responsible for the relationship you observe. You cannot rule out alternative explanations.

In a well-designed experiment with random assignment, participants are randomly placed into treatment and control groups. Random assignment distributes lurking variables roughly equally across groups, so if a difference is observed, the treatment is the most likely explanation.

The distinction: Observational = observe without intervening → can establish association only. Experiment with random assignment = assign treatments → can establish causation.

The gold standard: Causation requires a well-designed randomized controlled experiment. No observational study — however large, however strong the correlation — can establish causation alone.
🧠 Quick ID: Did the researcher randomly assign subjects to groups? Yes → experiment → causation possible. Did the researcher only observe and record? No assignment → observational → association only.

Graphs & Displays

S4 · S5 · 5 questions

The answer

Relative frequency: the proportion (or percentage) of the total that falls in this one category. Computed as: count for this category ÷ total count. Each row gets its own proportion.

Cumulative relative frequency: the running total — the proportion of data that falls in this category or any category before it. You add up relative frequencies as you move down the table. The last row always equals 1.0 (100%).

Think of cumulative as "how much has been accounted for so far."

🧠 The word "cumulative" means "building up." Regular relative frequency = this slice of the pie. Cumulative = this slice + everything before it. The cumulative column at the last row must always equal 1.0 — if it doesn't, something went wrong in your arithmetic.
Example: In a table of exam grades: Grade A = 12 students (RF = 0.30), Grade B = 18 students (RF = 0.45), Grade C = 10 students (RF = 0.25). Cumulative RF for Grade B = 0.30 + 0.45 = 0.75 → "75% of students earned a B or better."

The answer

Use relative frequencies whenever you are comparing groups of different sizes. Raw counts are misleading in those situations because a larger group will automatically produce larger counts even if the proportions are identical.

Example: You survey 30 vegetarians and 200 nonvegetarians about health outcomes. Finding that 25 nonvegetarians reported headaches vs. 5 vegetarians is not a fair comparison — the groups are completely different sizes. Converting to proportions (25/200 = 12.5% vs. 5/30 = 16.7%) tells a much more accurate story: vegetarians actually had a slightly higher headache rate.

Simple rule: Same-sized groups → raw counts are fine. Different-sized groups → always use relative frequencies (proportions or percentages) for fair comparison.
⚠️ Common mistake: Looking at a stacked bar chart and concluding "Group A has more of X" based on the visual height of a bar — without checking whether Group A is also larger overall. Always ask: "Are these groups the same size?"

The answer

The answer

A complete shape description covers four things: direction, modality, spread, and unusual features.

Direction / symmetry: Symmetric, left-skewed (long tail toward low values), right-skewed (long tail toward high values).

Modality: Unimodal (one peak), bimodal (two peaks — possible evidence of two subgroups in the data), multimodal, uniform (flat, no clear peak).

Unusual features: Outliers (values far from the bulk), gaps (regions with no data), clusters (multiple concentrated groups).

🧠 SOCS — a complete description framework:
Shape (symmetric, skewed left/right, bimodal…)
Outliers (any extreme values?)
Center (mean or median — which is appropriate?)
Spread (SD or IQR — which is appropriate?)
Always describe each in context — not just numbers, but what they mean for the real-world variable.
⚠️ Common mistake: Writing "the data is skewed" without specifying direction (left or right). The direction is essential — it tells you where the outliers are and whether mean or median is more appropriate. Also: always mention whether outliers or gaps are present, even if they're absent ("no outliers are apparent").

The answer

To read a percentile from a histogram, you are asking: "What fraction of the total area is to the LEFT of this value?" That fraction (as a percent) is the percentile.

For the maximum value: 100% of the data falls at or below the maximum. By definition, no value exceeds it — so the maximum is at the 100th percentile. This is always true, regardless of what the maximum value is.

To estimate from a histogram: add up the relative frequency (height × width for each bar) for all bars to the left of your target value. That sum is the approximate percentile.

🧠 Percentile = cumulative relative frequency up to that point. The 100th percentile doesn't mean "the best possible score" — it just means every single observation in the dataset is at or below that value. The maximum always earns the 100th percentile by definition.

Measures of Center, Spread & Shape

S6 · 7 questions

The answer

Symmetric distribution, no outliers → use Mean + Standard Deviation. The mean uses all data values equally and is the most precise summary when the distribution is balanced.

Skewed distribution OR outliers present → use Median + IQR. The median is resistant — extreme values can't pull it far from the center of the bulk of the data. The mean is pulled toward the tail; the median stays near the middle.

🧠 From the graph: See a long tail? The mean is chasing that tail — use median instead. See outliers? Same logic. See a bell curve shape with no unusual values? Mean and median will be close together — either works, but use mean + SD.
Why does skewness pull the mean? The mean is computed using every value — a single very large value adds significantly to the sum. The median only cares about position (middle value), not magnitude, so one extreme value can't move it far.
⚠️ Common mistake: Always reporting the mean because it "feels more official." If a distribution is right-skewed (like incomes or housing prices), the mean will be much higher than the typical value most people experience. In those cases, the median is the more honest description of center.
Try Exploratory Statistics Activity — see how mean and median shift with shape

The answer

Both measure spread, but they do so in fundamentally different ways — and one is far more sensitive to outliers than the other.

Standard Deviation (SD): measures the average distance of all values from the mean. Because it uses every data point, one extreme outlier can significantly inflate the SD. Paired with the mean.

IQR (Interquartile Range) = Q3 − Q1: measures the spread of the middle 50% of the data. It completely ignores the bottom 25% and top 25%, making it resistant to outliers. Paired with the median.

The pairing rule: Mean goes with SD. Median goes with IQR. They are paired because both are appropriate for the same conditions (symmetric vs. skewed/outlier-affected distributions). Never mix the pairs.
⚠️ Common mistake: Reporting the mean (which was inflated by outliers) alongside the IQR (which is resistant). This creates a contradictory summary — one number pretends outliers matter, the other ignores them. Pick the appropriate pair for the shape of your data and stick to it.

The answer

In general, yes — the mean gets pulled toward the tail more than the median. The pattern is:

Right-skewed (long tail toward high values): mean > median. The few very large values inflate the mean upward while the median stays near the bulk of the data.

Left-skewed (long tail toward low values): mean < median. A few very small values drag the mean down while the median holds its position.

However, this is a tendency, not an absolute law — the degree depends on how extreme the skew and outliers are. For very mildly skewed distributions, mean and median may be nearly identical.

🧠 Shortcut: "The mean chases the tail." In a right-skewed distribution, the tail points right → mean is pulled right → mean > median. In a left-skewed distribution → mean is pulled left → mean < median.

The answer

Lower Fence = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR

Upper Fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR

Any data value below the lower fence or above the upper fence is flagged as a potential outlier. On a boxplot, the whiskers extend to the most extreme values that are still within the fences — not to the fences themselves.

Step-by-step: (1) Find Q1 and Q3. (2) Compute IQR = Q3 − Q1. (3) Multiply IQR × 1.5. (4) Subtract from Q1 for the lower fence; add to Q3 for the upper fence. (5) Any data value outside those bounds is a potential outlier.

🧠 Example: Q1 = 20, Q3 = 35, IQR = 15. Lower fence = 20 − 1.5(15) = 20 − 22.5 = −2.5. Upper fence = 35 + 1.5(15) = 35 + 22.5 = 57.5. Any data point below −2.5 or above 57.5 is a potential outlier.
⚠️ Most common calculation error: Multiplying IQR × 1.5 correctly but then adding it to Q1 or subtracting from Q3 (the wrong quartile). Always: lower fence uses Q1 (subtract), upper fence uses Q3 (add). The fences extend outward from the middle 50%.

The answer

When n is odd: the median is the actual middle data value. Exclude it from both halves. Q1 is the median of the lower half; Q3 is the median of the upper half.

When n is even: the median is the average of the two middle values and is not an actual data point in the list. Split the data cleanly in half at the midpoint — no value to exclude. Q1 = median of the lower half; Q3 = median of the upper half.

🧠 Example (n = 9, odd): Data: 2 4 5 7 8 9 11 14 17. Median = 8 (excluded). Lower half = {2,4,5,7}, Q1 = (4+5)/2 = 4.5. Upper half = {9,11,14,17}, Q3 = (11+14)/2 = 12.5.

Example (n = 8, even): Data: 1 3 5 7 | 9 11 13 15. Lower half = {1,3,5,7}, Q1 = (3+5)/2 = 4. Upper half = {9,11,13,15}, Q3 = (11+13)/2 = 12.
Non-integer positions: If your position formula gives a decimal (e.g., position = 16.25), round up to the next whole number — use the 17th data value. This is the convention used in this course. Note that some software interpolates between values, which may give slightly different answers than your hand calculation.

The answer

The kth percentile is the value at or below which k% of the data falls.

So: if the 70th percentile is 6 cars, then 70% of people sold 6 or fewer cars. The correct interpretation uses "at or below" — the value itself counts.

Similarly: Q1 = 25th percentile means 25% of values are at or below Q1. Q3 = 75th percentile means 75% of values are at or below Q3. The median = 50th percentile means 50% fall at or below the median.

The wording that earns full credit: "The 70th percentile of [6 cars] means that approximately 70% of [salespeople] sold [6] or fewer cars." Include the variable, the value, and the population — all three.
⚠️ Common mistake: Writing "70% scored above" or "70% scored exactly 6." The percentile tells you how much data falls at or below the given value — not above it, and not exactly at it. If 30% is above, that's the complement: 100% − 70%.

The answer

Always interpret in context — a number alone is never a complete statistical answer in this course.

Center: "The median exam score is 78 points" → not enough. Better: "The typical student scored 78 points on the exam, meaning half of students scored 78 or below and half scored above."

Spread: "The IQR is 14 points" → not enough. Better: "The middle 50% of exam scores spanned a range of 14 points, indicating moderate variability among typical students."

Shape: "The distribution is right-skewed" → not enough. Better: "The distribution is right-skewed, suggesting that most students scored in the 60–80 range, but a few students scored exceptionally high, pulling the mean above the median."

🧠 Every numerical result needs three things: (1) The number with units. (2) What it measures. (3) What it means for the real-world variable and population. If any of those is missing, your answer is incomplete.

Z-Scores

S7 · 5 questions

The answer

The z-score formula: z = (x − μ) / σ

x — the individual data value you are standardizing

μ (mu) — the population mean (or sample mean x̄ if the population mean is unknown)

σ (sigma) — the population standard deviation (or sample SD s)

x − μ — the distance between your value and the mean (positive if above, negative if below)

÷ σ — converts that raw distance into a number of standard deviations

The result: how many standard deviations is x above (positive) or below (negative) the mean?

🧠 What z-scores do: They put values from completely different distributions onto the same common scale. A z-score of +2 means "two standard deviations above average" — whether you're measuring heights, scores, or temperatures. That's what makes z-scores so powerful for comparison.
⚠️ Common mistake: Plugging the standard deviation into the numerator or the mean into the denominator. Always: value minus mean on top; standard deviation on the bottom.

The answer

No — a negative z-score simply means the data value is below the mean. The sign tells you direction; it carries no value judgment on its own.

Positive z: the value is above the mean.

Negative z: the value is below the mean.

z = 0: the value equals the mean exactly.

Whether "below the mean" is good or bad depends entirely on context. A negative z-score for resting heart rate is a good thing (lower heart rate = better cardiovascular health). A negative z-score for exam scores might feel bad — but it's still just a position, not a verdict.

🧠 Template sentence: "A z-score of −1.8 means this value is 1.8 standard deviations below the mean." That's all it means — direction and distance. Nothing more.

The answer

In this course, the rule is: a z-score is considered unusual if |z| > 2 — that is, the value is more than 2 standard deviations from the mean in either direction.

|z| < 2 → Not unusual (typical range for most data in a roughly normal distribution)

|z| ≥ 2 → Unusual (in the outer ~5% of the distribution)

|z| ≥ 3 → Very unusual / likely an outlier

Justification sentence template: "A z-score of [z] is [unusual / not unusual] because it is [more than / less than] 2 standard deviations [above / below] the mean. Values this extreme occur in approximately [5% / 0.3%] of a normal distribution."
🧠 Why 2? In a normal distribution, about 95% of values fall within 2 standard deviations of the mean. So only about 5% of values — roughly 1 in 20 — have |z| > 2. That's rare enough to call "unusual."
⚠️ Common mistake: Writing "z = 1.9 is unusual" because it's close to 2. The threshold is |z| > 2. A z-score of 1.9 is "not unusual" — it barely falls within the typical range. Precision matters on the exam.

The answer

Rearrange the z-score formula to solve for x:

x = μ + z · σ

Plug in the z-score, mean (μ), and standard deviation (σ) and compute.

Built-in sanity check: positive z → x must be above the mean (x > μ). Negative z → x must be below the mean (x < μ). If your answer violates this, recheck your arithmetic.

🧠 Example: Mean = 70, SD = 8, z = −1.5. x = 70 + (−1.5)(8) = 70 − 12 = 58. Check: 58 < 70 ✓ (negative z means below the mean — correct).
⚠️ Common mistakes: (1) Subtracting z·σ from the mean when z is already negative — double-negating. Just compute z·σ as signed: (−1.5)(8) = −12, then add to μ: 70 + (−12) = 58. (2) Forgetting to multiply by σ — the formula is z·σ, not just z. Always include σ.

The answer

Raw values can't be compared across different distributions — a score of 85 on one exam and a score of 85 on another might represent very different relative performances if the exams had different means and spreads.

Z-scores fix this by converting everything to a common scale: standard deviations from the mean. To compare:

1. Calculate the z-score for each value using its own distribution's mean and SD.
2. Compare the z-scores — the higher z-score represents the more impressive performance relative to its distribution.
3. Explain in words: "Although [Person A] had a lower raw score, their z-score of [z₁] is higher than [Person B]'s z-score of [z₂], meaning [Person A] performed better relative to their group."

🧠 Example: Student A scored 90 on Test 1 (μ=80, σ=5): z = (90−80)/5 = +2.0. Student B scored 95 on Test 2 (μ=75, σ=15): z = (95−75)/15 = +1.33. Student A's z-score is higher → relative to their group, Student A outperformed Student B, even with a lower raw score.
